# ADR-0190: The journal records its decisions

- **Status:** Proposed
- **Date:** 2026-08-13

## Context

The control core (ADR-0149) journals admitted events and rebuilds its snapshot at boot by replaying them: for each record, decode the event, `reduce` it against the accumulating snapshot, and fold the resulting `Decisions` in with `Snapshot::apply`. The journal row is `(sequence, idempotency_key, event)` — the *fact* is durable, but the *decision* the reducer made about it at admission time is recorded nowhere. Replay therefore re-decides every event with whatever reducer is compiled into the currently running binary. Rejected events journal too, and must — the idempotency `seen` set is built from the journal, so a refused request's key has to persist for the dedup contract to hold — which means rows whose admitted outcome was "refused" sit in the replay stream alongside rows that were admitted.

`reduce` is pure and deterministic, but determinism holds within one binary, not across versions — and the admission rules change *deliberately*, because improving them is normal work. A live incident (2026-08-13, #4937) showed both failure directions in one store. A validation fix that loosened `ModelOverride::validate` (#4918) caused replay to re-decide a historically *rejected* seal as admitted: the refusal came back to life as an active bloom that held mainline advances. A door fix that tightened the landed-workpiece check (#4919) caused replay to re-refuse a historically *admitted* seal: every event downstream of it — a supersession, a land, the mainline observation — cascaded into refusals, and the replayed snapshot stranded its mainline on a digest weeks of history had already moved past. In both directions the rebuilt state contradicted what the coordinator had actually decided and acted on, including effects (pushed branches, merged pull requests, closed claims) that had long since happened in the world.

ADR-0187 names the sibling hazard one level down: persisted bytes cannot say what shape wrote them, so they record their writing schema. This is the same hazard for meaning rather than shape — a journaled event cannot say what the rules in force decided about it, so today its meaning is whatever the current binary re-derives, and history is silently rewritten every time the rules improve.

## Decision

Journal rows record the decision beside the event, and replay folds the record — it never re-decides.

1. **Admission decides once, durably.** The commit transaction already persists the event bytes, the membership mutations, and the outbox payloads atomically (ADR-0149 §The control core). It now also persists the reducer's `Decisions` — outcome plus ordered effects — on the journal row. `Decisions` already serializes; it feeds the transactional outbox today. The row becomes `(sequence, idempotency_key, event, outcome, effects, decider)`.
2. **Replay folds the record.** Boot replay becomes `apply(event, recorded_decisions)` per row, with no `reduce` call. A rejected row carries empty effects and folds nothing: resurrection of a refusal becomes unrepresentable while the journal still honestly records that the request was made and refused. A stricter door cannot un-admit history for the same reason — the admitted row's effects fold exactly as they did the day they were decided.
3. **Doors govern admissions, not history.** A rule change — looser or stricter — affects only events admitted after it ships. Reducers become safe to evolve, which is the property the pipeline needs to keep improving its own admission rules while carrying its own history.
4. **Each row stamps its decider.** The `decider` column records the identity of the build whose reducer made the decision — ADR-0187's stamp, applied to rules instead of schemas. An offline audit can re-reduce any row under the current binary and report divergence loudly ("today's reducer would have refused what row N admitted"), turning what is silent state rewriting today into a named report with a sequence number.
5. **Repairs are forward facts.** When a recorded decision turns out to have been wrong — the reducer had a bug, the rules were misconfigured — the correction is a new admitted event or an explicit, named migration over the store. It is never a side effect of upgrading the binary. Replay preserving a buggy decision's output is the accepted cost, and the point: the journal records what happened, bugs included, exactly as the git history of a repository records the commits that later needed reverting.

## Consequences

- The two incident classes become unrepresentable rather than merely fixed: a loosened rule cannot resurrect a refusal (empty effects fold nothing), and a tightened rule cannot cascade refusals through admitted history (recorded effects fold unconditionally).
- The journal becomes self-contained the way ADR-0187 makes sealed bytes self-contained: a row means what it says without reference to any particular binary. Forensics reads outcomes directly instead of reconstructing them by re-running a reducer of the right vintage.
- Observation-rule fixes (#4938) and every future door change ride on top safely — they change what gets admitted next, not what the store already holds.
- A store predating this decision backfills by replaying it once under the build that decided its rows and stamping each row with the decisions that replay actually produces; the proof of a faithful backfill is snapshot equivalence between the old binary's replay and the new binary's fold. The incident store was decided end-to-end by a single build, so its backfill is exact rather than reconstructed.
- Costs, accepted: effects bytes on every journal row (the same bounded vocabulary the outbox already serializes, paid once per event); a schema migration with a backfill step; and audits that *want* re-deciding must now ask for it explicitly instead of getting it implicitly at every boot.

## Alternatives considered

- **Refuse replay on reducer divergence** (record a rules fingerprint, abort loudly when it differs — ADR-0187's refusal shape without its record). Detection without recovery: every deliberate rule change would brick every store carrying affected history until a hand-written migration ships, making the common case — the rules improved — a boot failure. Divergence detection also requires re-deciding, which is the machinery this decision retires from the boot path; it survives as the offline audit in point 4.
- **Record only a rejected flag** (the minimal patch for the resurrection). Kills one direction and leaves the other: the incident's cascade came from a *stricter* door re-refusing an *admitted* event, which a flag on rejections does nothing for. Outcome plus effects covers both directions for the cost of columns the reducer already computes.
- **Snapshot checkpoints** (persist the snapshot periodically, replay only the tail). Shrinks the re-decide window without closing it — the tail still re-decides under the new binary, and the incident's resurrected seal was in the tail of any plausible checkpoint cadence. Checkpoints remain attractive for replay *cost* and compose with this decision; they do not substitute for it.
- **Never change admission rules on a live store** (pin the reducer, migrate stores across rule changes). Treats rule evolution as exceptional when it is the pipeline's own roadmap; every door improvement so far (#4918, #4919) would have required a store migration under this policy, and the migrations themselves would need exactly the outcome records this decision adds.
- **Event-sourcing purism** — "events are facts; state is always derivable by folding the current reducer over them." Sound only while the fold function is immutable. Ours is deliberately versioned, so the derivation is not a function of the journal alone but of (journal, binary) — and the binary half was unrecorded. Recording the decision restores the property purism assumes: the store alone determines the state.
